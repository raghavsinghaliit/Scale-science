Why companies miss what they miss

Not because they are careless. Because they are human.

We will go to great lengths to defend a belief, and almost no length at all to find out whether it is true. It is visible in politics and in personal life. It is more surprising in business, which is punished for being wrong. And yet most companies rarely apply real rigour to how they form beliefs and spend money.

The narrative default

Our dominant way of making sense of the world is not investigation. It is story. We build explanations that feel coherent, fit what the people around us believe, and then interpret new evidence through them. That machinery does not switch off when we walk into an office. Most of what passes for thinking in organisations is rationalisation: fast, fluent, and working backwards from a conclusion already reached.

Survival before depth

Human instinct optimises for safety, speed and belonging, not statistical truth. Inside a company, that instinct finds a home. A wrong judgement call gets the benefit of the doubt; it was a reasonable view at the time. A rigorous analysis that turns out wrong has no graceful exit. So judgement is not just easier than rigour. It is politically safer. And investigation threatens hierarchy: when the answer comes from the method, it no longer comes from the most senior person in the room.

What gets overlooked

  • Diminishing returns. The next rupee is assumed to work like the last one. It rarely does.
  • Cannibalisation. Growth in one channel is often demand moved from another pocket, counted as a win.
  • The background. Customers who would have bought anyway get credited to whatever ran that week.
  • The long-term excuse. Effects decay fast; most impact arrives in weeks or not at all. Difficulty of measurement is not evidence of impact.
  • Interactions. Every lever changes the returns of every other, so individually good decisions add up to a poor whole.
  • Averages. Strong and wasteful activities blend into one acceptable number, and the waste hides inside it.

When reality is quiet, institutions speak

Where feedback is weak, people reach for substitutes: awards, case studies, peer validation, the confidence of whoever called it first. And when anyone pushes back, there is the final refuge: the claim that it is all too complex to measure. Almost every complex system has parts that can be measured and tested. Declaring the whole thing too complex simply leaves judgement as the only option, which happens to be the one where being wrong costs the least.

Why it matters

An outstanding business might earn ten to fifteen percent on its capital. The gap between excellence and mediocrity is a few points. A few points of quiet, compounding misallocation is that gap. And without real feedback, a company can over-invest in something tenfold, or under-invest by the same margin, and never know.

The data exists. The methods exist. What is missing is the habit of asking: what would have happened anyway?

How we work differently

We start with what is actually known, not believed. We name the assumptions underneath every decision, test the ones that matter, and sharpen the rest until they are precise enough to act on. When an outcome disappoints, we can see which assumption failed, so the next decision starts from a better foundation instead of from scratch.
