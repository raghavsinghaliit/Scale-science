Growth Engine Design

Growth is capital allocation under uncertainty. Design the engine, and the trajectory follows.

Growth in an internet company runs on perpetual engines — systems that take in resources every day and turn them into acquisition, retention and usage. They are the lifeblood of the company, and most are built by accident.

Two resources, both finite

  • Capital — spent on advertising, referrals, cashbacks and pricing.
  • User attention — captured on-property through merchandising and native discovery, and off-property through CRM: notifications and messages.

Both are limited — every day and in total. So the rate at which you convert them into output, the yield, dictates your growth trajectory. And yield is decided by engine design: how and where each rupee and each impression is used to maximise company value.

Why it can’t be fluked

There is no clean playbook for engine design, and it isn’t taught anywhere. Getting it right needs incrementality, elasticity, portfolio optimisation and profiling. The difference between a well-built engine and a jury-rigged one is not a few percent — it can be multiples.

Intelligent, well-credentialed teams still default to judgement and narrative. But allocating across many levers — each with non-linear returns, each affecting the others — is a closed mathematical problem. Intuition is valuable for strategy. It cannot allocate efficiently across many variables.

Discovery, then allocation

Discovery is understanding what each lever truly costs and truly delivers: where the bottlenecks are, where the elasticity is, and how to separate the impact of one lever from another. Allocation is placing each successive rupee where it earns the most — until the marginal return on every lever is roughly equal.

In a well-run engine, moving money from one lever to another changes nothing. If it changes a lot, the engine is out of balance.

Where engines leak

  • Imbalance — spend isn’t equalised by incremental return, so revenue is left on the table without any change in budget.
  • Blindness — cannibalisation, lifetime value and true attribution go unmeasured, so decisions run on incomplete information.
  • Mixing — things with different economics are averaged together, and waste hides inside a healthy-looking number.
